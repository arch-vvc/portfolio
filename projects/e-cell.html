Business crisis simulation

PESU Entrepreneurship Cell, Organising Committee

The problem

Case competitions hand students one polished case and a deck template. Real managers get a crisis they did not pick, with half the facts, and have to decide anyway.

What it is

A Jeopardy style board of 20 corporate crises for teams to pick from, run end to end for 100+ participants. The cases sit on four tiers, from everyday office problems up to the crises that can end a company. Behind the board is a bank of 80+ written cases.

Every case has the same shape: a scenario, the key challenges, a real precedent from a company that lived through something similar, and discussion points that push a team toward a decision.

Three example cases

1. Company saving M&A falls through

Scenario. Your company has struggled to keep up in a fast industry. After months of talks, a merger with a much larger player was set to turn things around and was hailed internally as the deal of the decade. At the eleventh hour it collapses over valuation, integration and culture. The board is split, outside investors have pulled out, employees are demoralised and competitors are watching.

Precedent. In 2008 Microsoft offered $44.6 billion for Yahoo. Yahoo said the bid undervalued it and the deal died. Yahoo kept declining and was later sold to Verizon for a fraction of that offer, while Microsoft turned to cloud computing.

The team is asked

  • How do you restore investor and market confidence after a deal this public collapses?
  • What do you tell employees today to steady morale?
  • What capital or partnerships can you look at now, and what is the new roadmap?

2. Death of the CEO or co founder with an IPO imminent

Scenario. InnovativeTech, an AI healthcare company worth a billion dollars, is a month from its IPO. Its founder and CEO dies of a heart attack. He made every major decision, never named a successor and never wrote down his long term vision. Investors are jittery, the press is speculating and power struggles start inside the executive team.

Precedent. When Steve Jobs died in 2011, Apple faced the same doubts about whether Tim Cook could hold its direction. The handover was uncertain at first and worked out.

The team is asked

  • Do you go ahead with the IPO or delay it, and how do you tell investors?
  • Who leads in the meantime, and what should the permanent leader look like?
  • How do you honour the founder while keeping staff, partners and the press steady?

3. Payment page breakdown

Scenario. You run an e-commerce platform known for a smooth experience. Just before the holiday season the payment page starts throwing errors and transactions fail. Support is swamped, social media is full of complaints and lost sales threaten the revenue plan.

Precedent. In 2020 several major retailers had payment failures on Black Friday, with lost sales and angry customers.

The team is asked

  • What do you do in the first hour to cut the flood of support requests?
  • How do you find and fix the fault, and what backup way to pay can go live now?
  • What do you change so a critical page like this is tested and reliable from here on?

Each case in the bank also lists its key challenges and a longer account of the precedent.
